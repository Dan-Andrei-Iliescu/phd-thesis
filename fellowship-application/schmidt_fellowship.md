Positive Psychology studies how to improve an individual's lifetime welbeing.

Buffering theory is the hypothesis that some specific mental behaviours that can be learned can safeguard an individual against mental illness or decreased welbeing due to negative life events.

Buffering theory has a huge potential for impact on people's robustness to traumatic life events.

Is that true? Can good mental habits safeguard our minds (and bodies) against trauma?

For example, there is evidence that cultivating optimism can reduce the chances of depression in later life.

Csikszentmihaly [[Seligman & Csikszentmihaly, 2000](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2000-13324-001)]

In fact, when it comes to individuals, apart from extreme situations, most of the treatments that we can perform upon ourselves are behaviours and habits rather than pharmacological interventions. Things like diet, activities, sleep schedules, mental habits.

But there is a problem. Running clinical trials (the method of choice in medicine) for lifetime events is, by definition, very slow. The events that we are interested in take a lifetime before we can see their effects.

This is complicated by the problem that, unlike pharmacological treatments, it is difficult to administer the treatment directly or to force participants to have certain mental habits.

What we're left with is observational data. Scientists have access to large amounts of observational data from the user.

## How to define generalization on holdout sets?

It is well established that missingness depending on unobserved variables leads to an biased estimate of 

*Missing data in predictor variables also do not cause bias in analyses of complete cases if the reasons for the missing data are unrelated to the outcome.*

The example they talk about goes something like this: You have three binary variables, high blood pressure, fever and hospitalization. Blood pressure has missing values. If the missingness in blood pressure is only due to fever, and hospitalizations only depend on fever, then estimating $p(h | f)$ will be unbiased.

$a$ is unbiased for $b$ if $\mathbb{E} [a] = b$.

### Elements of statistical learning

$\mathcal{T} = (X, Y)$ is a random variable controlling something like "which of the infinite available training sets is this training set?"
Prediction error over a test sample

$$\mathbb{E}_{X', Y'} [L(Y', \hat{f} (X')) | \mathcal{T} = (x, y)]$$

which is in our case

$$\mathbb{E}_{X', Y'} [ - \log \mathrm{Pr}_\theta (Y' | X') | \mathcal{T} = (x, y)]$$

A related quantity is the expected prediction error:

$$\mathbb{E}_{X', Y', \mathcal{T}} [ - \log \mathrm{Pr}_\theta (Y' | X') | \mathcal{T}]$$

Bias is $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}} [(\hat{f} (x') - f(x'))^2]$, Variance is $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}} [(\hat{f} (x') - \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}[\hat{f} (x')])^2]$

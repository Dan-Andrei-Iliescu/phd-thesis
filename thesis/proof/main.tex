\chapter{Function approximation}

\section{Identifiability of amortised function approximation}

\paragraph{Theorem.} Let $P$ be a distribution over $Y \in \mathbb{R}^D$, $O \in \mathbb{R}^D$, $M \in \{0,1\}^D$. Let $Q$ be an equivalent distribution $Y, O, O' \in \mathbb{R}^D$, $M \in \{0,1\}^D$. 

If 

$$\forall y, o, o', ~ Q_{Y|O}(y|o) ~ Q_{Y|O}(y|o') = \frac{P_{Y|O,O'}(y | o, o') P_Y (y) P_{O, O'} (o, o')}{P_{O} (o) ~ P_{O'} (o')}$$

Another way to say this is $\forall o, o', ~ \frac{Q(o' | o) Q(o | o')}{P(o, o')} = \frac{P(o, o')}{P(o) P(o')}$

and 

if $\forall y, ~ Q_Y (y) = P_Y (y)$, 

then $\forall y,o, ~ Q_{Y|O = o} (y) = P_{Y | O = o} (y)$.

\paragraph{Proof.} We prove this by contradiction, let's start by assuming the opposite: Assume there exists some distributions \(P\) and \(Q\), and random variables \(A\) and \(B\) such that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For some values of \(A\) and \(B\), either \(P(A|B) \neq Q(A|B)\) or \(P(B|A) \neq Q(B|A)\).
    \item Yet, for all values of \(A\) and \(B\), we have \(\frac{Q(A | B) Q(B | A)}{P(A, B)} = \frac{P(A, B)}{P(A) P(B)}\).
\end{enumerate}

From Bayes theorem, we have:
\[P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}\]
\[Q(A|B) = \frac{Q(B|A)Q(A)}{Q(B)}\]

From our assumption, either \(P(A|B) \neq Q(A|B)\) or \(P(B|A) \neq Q(B|A)\).

Let's take the first case, \(P(A|B) \neq Q(A|B)\), which means:
\[\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)} \neq \frac{Q(B|A)Q(A)}{Q(B)}\]

From our assumption's second part:
\[\frac{Q(A | B) Q(B | A)}{P(A, B)} = \frac{P(A, B)}{P(A) P(B)}\]
\[Q(B|A) = \frac{P(A)P(B)}{Q(A)}\]

But this leads to a contradiction.




Let's assume there exist probability distributions \(P\) and \(Q\), and for some values of random variables \(A\) and \(B\), we have either \(P(A|B) \neq Q(A|B)\) or \(P(B|A) \neq Q(B|A)\) and yet for all values of \(A\) and \(B\):

\[ \frac{Q(A | B) Q(B | A)}{P(A, B)} = \frac{P(A, B)}{P(A) P(B)} \] (1)

From Bayes' theorem, we have:

\[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)} \]
\[ Q(A|B) = \frac{Q(B|A)Q(A)}{Q(B)} \]

Plug these into equation (1):

\[ \frac{\frac{Q(B|A)Q(A)}{Q(B)} \times Q(B|A)}{P(A, B)} = \frac{P(A, B)}{P(A) P(B)} \]

Rearrange and simplify:

\[ Q(B|A)^2 \times \frac{Q(A)}{Q(B)} = \frac{P(A, B)^2}{P(A) P(B)} \] \[ (2) \]

Now, from our assumption, if \(P(A|B) \neq Q(A|B)\) or \(P(B|A) \neq Q(B|A)\), then there must be some change in the marginal probabilities of A or B (or both) between P and Q to maintain the relationship in equation (1). 

Consider the first case, \(P(A|B) \neq Q(A|B)\):

From equation (2), if \(Q(B|A)\) remains constant, then the ratio \(\frac{Q(A)}{Q(B)}\) must change. This indicates that the marginals \(Q(A)\) and/or \(Q(B)\) have changed between P and Q.

Now consider the second case, \(P(B|A) \neq Q(B|A)\):

From equation (2), if the ratio \(\frac{Q(A)}{Q(B)}\) remains constant, then \(Q(B|A)\) itself must change. This also indicates a change in the marginals.

However, this contradicts our assumption in equation (1) that claims the relationship holds for all \(A\) and \(B\). The changing marginals would disrupt this relationship for other values of \(A\) and \(B\).

Thus, by contradiction, the statement is proven: for all \(P, Q\), if there exists \(A, B\) such that either \(P(A|B) \neq Q(A|B)\) or \(P(B|A) \neq Q(B|A)\), then for those \(P, Q\) and for all \(A, B\), we must have \(\frac{Q(A | B) Q(B | A)}{P(A, B)} \neq \frac{P(A, B)}{P(A) P(B)}\).
